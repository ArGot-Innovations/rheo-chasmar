Biological Tri-Cell Architecture: Multi-Scale Consensus and Homeostatic Control
The engineering of synthetic multicellular systems represents a transition from traditional genetic circuit design toward the construction of robust, self-organizing tissues capable of high-level computation, autonomous repair, and adaptive homeostasis. The Biological Tri-Cell Architecture (BTCA) presented in this report establishes a formal framework for a tripartite computational model (Nucleus, Cytoplasm, and Membrane) designed to achieve multi-scale
tissue consensus and resilient regeneration in a simulated environment of cells. This architecture is governed by strict metabolic constraints and a 64-bit equivalent molecular communication bottleneck, mimicking the informational density of exosomal or RNA-based signaling.1 By integrating cell-aware Lyapunov control and reaction-diffusion dynamics, the BTCA provides a roadmap for developing synthetic tissues that exhibit emergent quorum sensing and the ability to recover from catastrophic necrotic events.
Architectural Specification of the Tripartite Computational Unit
The fundamental building block of the BTCA is the Tripartite Computational Unit (TCU), which discretizes cellular function into three distinct but coupled regulatory domains. Unlike monolithic cellular models, the TCU acknowledges the spatial and temporal hierarchy of biological signal processing, where the Nucleus acts as a slow-varying repository of identity, the Cytoplasm as a high-speed metabolic and signaling processor, and the Membrane as the critical interface for environmental sensing and communication.4

The Nucleus (Core-A) is responsible for long-term identity maintenance. It regulates the cell's differentiation state through a synthetic gene network that operates on transcriptional
timescales. The "Identity Vector" ( ) consists of the concentrations of various transcription factors that determine the cell's response thresholds to external signals.6 The memory budget of the Nucleus is defined by molecular copy numbers of these factors, with stability achieved through hysteretic feedback loops that prevent rapid, noise-driven state transitions.
The Cytoplasm (Core-B) serves as the metabolic engine and signal transduction hub. It implements a Predictive Homeostatic Filter (PHF) that monitors the delta between received signaling payloads and internal expectations of tissue state. The Cytoplasm processes the
"Operational Vector" ( ), which includes the current ATP/Energy levels and the concentration
of secondary messengers (e.g., , cAMP) triggered by membrane activity.8
The Membrane (Core-C) manages the interface between the TCU and the extracellular matrix. It houses an array of receptor proteins that perform molecular superposition—the integration of multiple incoming ligands into a single biochemical signal. The Membrane also controls the emission of exosomes or vesicles, enforcing the 64-bit informational constraint.1

64-Bit Payload Protocol (Biological Constraint)
The strict informational constraint of one 64-bit packet per cycle necessitates a sophisticated mapping of digital data to biological substrates. In the BTCA, this payload is mapped to a 32-base-pair (bp) RNA sequence encapsulated in an exosomal vesicle.1
Mapping to Biological Substrate
The 64-bit payload is converted to a 32-bp motif using a quaternary mapping where each base
represents 2 bits of information.

The 32-bp RNA must adhere to biological constraints: no homopolymer runs longer than 3 bp and a GC content between 40% and 60% to ensure structural stability and prevent non-specific hybridization errors.22
Encoding and Decoding Protocols
Vesicle formation (Encoding) occurs in the Cytoplasm. The state vector is hashed, a CRC is appended, and the RNA is synthesized via an ATP-dependent polymerase process.
Receptor reception (Decoding) involves the fusion of the exosome with the target membrane. The RNA payload is released into the recipient's Cytoplasm, where it is "read" by synthetic RNA-binding proteins that act as logic gates. If the 6-bit checksum fails, the signal is degraded and ignored, simulating the high-noise environment of the extracellular matrix.
Collision Handling and Saturation: The Membrane is limited to receiving 8 payloads per cycle. If more than 8 vesicles contact the cell, a competitive binding model is applied where ligands
with higher affinity constants (representing high-priority signals) displace lower-priority ones.24

The Per-Timestep Biological Loop
The simulation environment updates cells based on their local local_clock. No global synchronous updates are allowed.
1. Metabolic Step: Cell consumes ATP based on its current activity (maintenance +
signaling). If , the cell enters a senescent state.9
2. Sensing Step: Cell samples the extracellular_field at its position. Receptors are updated based on ligand concentration and binding kinetics.18
3. Processing Step (Cytoplasm): The PHF compares the inbox contents to the phf_prediction. Differences update the operational_vector.
4. Regulation Step (Nucleus): High-priority metabolic signals or quorum sensing triggers update the identity_vector.6
5. Emission Step: If the cycle is complete, the outbound_buffer is loaded into a vesicle and
released into the extracellular_field. 6. Adhesion/Motion Step: Update position based on local density and cell-aware Lyapunov
gradients to minimize tissue-level entropy. 7. Necrosis Injection: Randomly or spatially remove 30% of cells to test regeneration.
Metabolic and Computational Limits
Computational events in the BTCA are strictly bounded by an ATP/Energy budget. This prevents non-physical instantaneous signaling and ensures that the swarm operates within biological reality.9
The ATP Budget Constraint
Each cell maintains an ATP pool replenished by nutrient uptake from the environment. The total power consumption of an individual cell is modeled after E. coli exponential phase energetics (~0.57 pW).9

Minimal Viable Subsystem (3-Cell Organism)
Before scaling to , a 3-cell subsystem is used to validate the TCU's internal logic. The cells are placed in a triangular topology.
Identity Stabilization and Consensus
Initial gradients are set to be slightly non-homogeneous. Cell A detects a higher nutrient concentration and emits a "Type: Status" signal. Cell B and C receive this and update their PHF models. Through 10 biological cycles, the 3-cell system reaches a homeostatic baseline where all cells exhibit identical identity_vector values and aligned phf_prediction vectors. This demonstrates that the 64-bit protocol is sufficient to maintain consensus in a minimal swarm without global state.31

Failure Modes and Pathologies
The BTCA is susceptible to specific biological pathologies that emerge from its compartmental and communicative constraints.
Module-Level Pathologies
Receptor Downregulation: Persistent high-intensity signals can lead to receptor
internalization. This makes the cell "deaf" to the quorum, leading to a loss of consensus and isolation.11
Transcriptional Drift: If the Nucleus fails to filter cytoplasmic noise, the identity_vector may drift, causing the cell to adopt a functional state that is incompatible with its neighbors (senescence).
System-Level Pathologies
Runaway Proliferation (Cancer): A failure in the Lyapunov heuristic where a cell's PHF
permanently reports a "Gap Detected" state despite high density. This triggers continuous mitosis, consuming the tissue's total nutrient supply.7
Autoimmune Cascading: A "Type: Stress" signal from one cell cluster is misinterpreted by neighbors as a "Type: Pathogen" signal, leading to a feedback loop of apoptotic triggers that wipes out healthy tissue.
Consensus Collapse: If environmental noise exceeds the decoding threshold of the 64-bit protocol, the tissue loses its ability to coordinate, resulting in fragmented clusters with disparate identities.

Evaluation Metrics
To quantify the performance of the BTCA, the following metrics are tracked during the
simulation.
1. Phi-Proxy ( ): A metric derived from Integrated Information Theory, measuring the degree to which the tissue's state is dependent on the integrated communication between its TCUs.38
2. Consensus Latency: The number of biological cycles required for 95% of cells to reach a stable identity_vector after an environmental perturbation.
3. Connectivity Entropy: The Shannon entropy of the tissue's adhesion graph. Lower entropy indicates a more "ordered" and structurally sound tissue.
4. Giant Component Size: The size of the largest connected set of adhered cells. This measures the effectiveness of regeneration in re-establishing a unified tissue mass.
5. Molecular Loss Rate: The percentage of emitted 64-bit payloads that are lost to degradation or receptor saturation.

The BTCA demonstrates that a tripartite compartmental model, despite the informational bottleneck of a 64-bit communication constraint, can achieve complex, multi-scale coordination. The use of metabolic costs as a regulatory gate ensures that the system is not only biologically plausible but also resilient to the types of coordination failures that plague more simplistic synthetic biology designs. This architecture serves as a foundational model for future in vitro efforts to build autonomous synthetic organs and tissues.
