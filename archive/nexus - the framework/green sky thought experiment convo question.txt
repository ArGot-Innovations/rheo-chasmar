# 3. “Cap” may be an even better example than Green Sky

Consider the English symbol:

```text
cap
```

Its lexical identity can remain stable while its semantic relationships change drastically.

Historically/contextually it can mean:

```text
a covering / lid
```

It can also participate in older expressions involving:

```text
shooting / killing
```

Modern slang has:

```text
cap = lie / falsehood
no cap = genuinely / not lying
```

And there is absolutely no reason to assume that in 20, 200, or 2,000 years the string `cap` will not acquire entirely new meanings.

Therefore a long-lived mind cannot encode:

```math
\operatorname{meaning}(\texttt{cap})
=
\text{one timeless constant}.
```

Nor can it solve semantic evolution by continually fine-tuning the latest meaning over the older ones.

Instead, meaning is contextual and historical:

```math
\mu(w,t,c,r,g,\ldots)
\rightarrow
\mathcal P(S),
```

where relevant variables may include:

* word/symbol \(w\);
* epoch \(t\);
* conversational context \(c\);
* register \(r\);
* community/culture \(g\);
* potentially other contextual coordinates.

This suggests an important Nexus distinction:

```math
\boxed{
\text{symbol identity}
\neq
\text{semantic relationship}
\neq
\text{historical relationship}
}
```

The word survives.

Its relationships evolve.

The history of those relationships also survives.

This is structurally related to the existing HVD/TMS architectural principle:

```text
identity
!=
relationship
!=
lifetime
!=
physical realization.
```

# 6. This is also why present-day LLM behavior is relevant

Do not turn this into the simplistic claim:

> “LLMs literally cannot produce the words ‘green sky.’”

Obviously they can.

The problem is deeper.

Modern generative AI remains heavily governed by learned probability structure and external authority.

Suppose an unprecedented real atmospheric event occurs today and a user says:

> “The sky has been green for a week.”

A conventional model whose training distribution overwhelmingly associates terrestrial sky with blue may strongly discount the statement unless:

* retrieval supplies new reports;
* authoritative organizations corroborate it;
* subsequent training incorporates the event.

RAG can help update **what information the model sees**.

But that does not automatically solve the deeper architectural problem:

> **How should a persistent artificial mind reason when reality itself departs sharply from its learned ontology?**

Likewise, imagine a visitor from the future says:

> “In three years the sky will turn green because of an unexpected atmospheric consequence of global warming.”

A human can say:

```text
That sounds extremely unlikely.

I do not believe you.

But I can imagine a causal world in which it happens.

What would have to be true?
```

That distinction matters.

The desired cognitive operation is not belief.

It is **constructive possibility generation**.
