## 2. Foundational Calculus of HTLA: A Review of Linear Principles

HTLA serves as the necessary linear foundation for HTNLA.

### 2.1 Hypervector Spaces and Direct Sum Notation
The core data structure is the **hypervector**, H. It is a composite entity formally defined as a tuple of vectors, H = (H_ℓ)_{ℓ ∈ L}, where each H_ℓ resides in a distinct, labeled vector space ℝ^{n_ℓ}. The structure is represented by the direct sum notation:

H = ⨁_{ℓ ∈ L} ℝ^{n_ℓ}

This direct sum preserves the identity and dimensionality of each labeled component, treating multi-modal information as a single algebraic entity without losing its structural and semantic meaning.


Example (labeled components): NeurocognitiveState = {neurochem: R^6, conscious: R^5}. This enables distinct, label-specific nonlinearities (sigma_neurochem vs. sigma_conscious) within NTNs.

### 2.2 Transdimensional Operators as Block Matrices
Operations between hypervector spaces are mediated by **Transdimensional Operators**. A linear map, L: H_s → H_t, transforming a source hypervector H_s into a target hypervector H_t, is defined as a block matrix, L = [L_{t,s}]_{t ∈ T, s ∈ S}.

Each block, L_{t,s} ∈ ℝ^{q_t × p_s}, functions as a standard linear map, allowing for "seamless transitions and interactions" between components of varying dimensions.

### 2.3 The Graded Inner Product and Label Kernels
Similarity is quantified by the **Graded Inner Product**, defined as ⟨H, K⟩_w = ∑_{ℓ ∈ L} w_ℓ ⟨H_ℓ, K_ℓ⟩. A key innovation is the **label kernel**, w = {w_ℓ}_{ℓ ∈ L}, a set of weights assigned to each dimension label to explicitly encode the semantic or contextual importance of different data modalities.

### 2.4 Core Theoretical Guarantees of HTLA
The robustness of HTLA is underpinned by two guarantees:

*   **Concentration of Measure theorem:** Proves that the inner product of high-dimensional, random vectors deviates minimally from its mean, ensuring that small perturbations or noise do not drastically alter similarity.
*   **Operator Convergence theorem:** Ensures that sequences of HTLA operators refined during iterative learning processes will converge to stable solutions rather than diverging.

## 3. Formalizing Hypervector Nonlinear Transdimensional Algebra (HTNLA)

HTNLA extends HTLA to incorporate nonlinear operations, allowing the system to model non-convex relationships.

### 3.1 Nonlinear Transdimensional Networks (NTNs)
HTNLA introduces **Nonlinear Transdimensional Networks (NTNs)** as the primary mechanism for nonlinear transformations.

The mathematical formulation for a single NTN layer is defined as:

H' = σ(M · H)

where σ: ℝ^d → ℝ^d is a component-wise activation function. The block-wise operation is detailed as:

H'_{ℓ'} = σ_{ℓ'}(∑_{ℓ ∈ L} L_{ℓ',ℓ} H_ℓ + b_{ℓ'})

where L_{ℓ',ℓ} is a linear block operator, and b_{ℓ'} is a bias vector.

The framework supports a multi-layer extension, H^{(k+1)} = σ^{(k)}(M^{(k)} · H^{(k)}), with an optional **skip connection** to ensure stability and prevent vanishing gradients during training.

### 3.2 Activation Function Selection
The choice of σ is critical and should align with computational efficiency and cognitive dynamics.

| Activation Function | Properties | Implications |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **ReLU** | Computationally efficient, induces sparsity. | Breaks linearity, makes unbinding non-invertible. |
| **Swish/GELU** | Smooth, continuous derivatives. | Offers more stable training dynamics. |
| **tanh/sigmoid** | Used in bio-inspired loops (CA, HTCE). | Crucial for modeling neuromodulatory feedback. |
| **k-WTA** | Enforces sparsity constraint (e.g., 70%). | Acts as stabilizing force and implicit regularization. |

### 3.3 The Evolved Algebra of HTNLA
The introduction of nonlinearities fundamentally alters the algebraic properties, transforming HTLA's graded ℝ-algebra into a **graded nonlinear algebra**.

*   **Loss of Invertibility:** The application of a nonlinear activation function breaks the linear structure and makes operations like **unbind non-invertible in a direct sense**. This necessitates the use of approximate inversion methods like gradient ascent or relying on invertible activations like LeakyReLU.
*   **Nonlinear Binding:** HTNLA formalizes a nonlinear binding operation to enhance expressivity while preserving holographic properties:

    bind(H, K) = tanh(H ⊙ K + b)

    where ⊙ is circular convolution and b is a learnable bias hypervector.
*   **Dimensional Adaptation (Nonlinear Projections):** Nonlinear projections, H'_ℓ = σ(P_ℓ H_ℓ), where P_ℓ is a learned matrix, allow for dynamic management of labels and dimensions as new data modalities are introduced or refined. This is a powerful mechanism for managing time-varying hypervector space H(t) = ⨁_{ℓ ∈ L(t)} ℝ^{n_ℓ(t)}.


*   Equivariant Nonlinearities by Label: For labels with known symmetry (e.g., rotations/translations in images), use group-equivariant activations/blocks to preserve invariants and improve sample efficiency.

### 3.4 Theoretical Guarantees and Statistical Properties in HTNLA
The move to a nonlinear framework requires a re-evaluation of guarantees.

*   **Sparsity and Concentration:** Sparsity induction via a **k-WTA (k-Winners-Take-All) algorithm** (enforcing a **70% sparsity constraint**) acts as a stabilizing force and preserves the Concentration of Measure property. The geometry of sparse vectors is well-behaved, and their inner products concentrate effectively, re-establishing holographic properties.
*   **Computational Efficiency:** The 70% sparsity constraint reduces the computational cost from a prohibitive O(D^2) to a more manageable **O(D log D) per layer** for high dimensions.

## 4. Architectural and Algorithmic Integration with the HVD Stack

Integration requires modifying the HVD architecture across three layers: API → TMS → HDF5.

### 4.1 The Evolved HVD API and TMS
*   **API Extension:** The HVD API must be extended with new endpoints, such as `associate_nonlinear(query_hv, layers=2)`, while preserving existing HTLA endpoints via feature flags for backward compatibility.

### 4.2 Deferral of Nonlinear Computation
To maintain the HVD's low-latency performance, computationally expensive nonlinear operations are strategically deferred.

*   **Deferred Transformation:** New engram operations are a fast, linear write to the Write-Ahead Log (WAL) and in-memory Memtable. The non-linear transformation is deferred to a **non-blocking Compaction process** that runs in the background.
*   **Consolidated State:** The system's on-disk **SSTables contain a consolidated, non-linear representation** of the memory. This architectural choice mirrors the biological brain's two-tiered memory system (fast working memory → slow long-term memory).

### 4.3 Adapting the Active State Cache: HNSW Indexing
The Active State Cache, built on a Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) graph, must be adapted to the nonlinear space.

*   **Nonlinear Query Routing:** A query vector (`query_hv`) is transformed nonlinearly by NTN layers *before* it is used to search the HNSW index. This allows the search algorithm to effectively navigate the **manifold curvature** of the hypervector space.
*   **Metric Challenge:** The primary challenge is adapting the d_HTLA distance metric to this nonlinear space, which requires a re-derivation of the distance function to account for the transformations. Research into developing a novel metric that respects nonlinear transformations is a high priority.


*   Hybrid Indexing at Scale: Pair HNSW with IVF (inverted file) partitioning and PQ (product quantization) on cold, consolidated nonlinear embeddings to reduce search space and memory while preserving recall.

### 4.4 Distributed HTNLA and Manifold-Aware Sharding
*   **Consistency:** The learned NTN weights must be kept in a consistent state across all nodes, requiring a **Raft-based consensus mechanism** to ensure strong consistency and fault tolerance in a distributed environment.
*   **Manifold-Aware Sharding:** The application of NTN layers to the query vector before fan-out enables the system to implicitly learn to group semantically similar vectors, even if they are far apart in the original linear space. This would make Approximate Nearest Neighbor Search (ANNS) more efficient and accurate by ensuring that related concepts are physically collocated.

### 8.4 Code Implementation Examples

#### Basic HyperVector and Nonlinear Operator

```python
import numpy as np

class HyperVector:
    def __init__(self, components: dict):
        self.components = components  # {label: vector}

class NonlinearBlockOperator:
    def __init__(self, blocks: dict):
        self.blocks = blocks  # {output_label: {input_label: matrix}}

    def apply_nonlinear(self, hypervector: HyperVector, activation=np.maximum) -> HyperVector:
        linear_output = self.apply(hypervector)
        nonlinear_components = {label: activation(0, vec) for label, vec in linear_output.components.items()}
        return HyperVector(nonlinear_components)
```

    def k_wta_activation(vector: np.ndarray, k_factor: float) -> np.ndarray:

import pymc as pm
import arviz as az
import numpy as np

# -------------------------------
# INPUT: Observed fidelity vector
# -------------------------------
F = np.array([0.91, 0.88, 0.94, 0.90])  # example data

# -------------------------------
# MODEL
# -------------------------------
with pm.Model() as model:
    # Priors for hypothesis H
    # Use a Bernoulli prior on H with prob pi
    pi = pm.Beta("pi", alpha=1, beta=1)  # uninformative prior for P(H=1)
    H = pm.Bernoulli("H", p=pi)

    # Priors for Beta distribution parameters conditioned on H
    # Use hierarchical priors so parameters differ if H=1 vs H=0
    alpha_pres = pm.Gamma("alpha_pres", alpha=2, beta=0.5)
    beta_pres  = pm.Gamma("beta_pres",  alpha=2, beta=0.5)
    alpha_fail = pm.Gamma("alpha_fail", alpha=2, beta=0.5)
    beta_fail  = pm.Gamma("beta_fail",  alpha=2, beta=0.5)

    # Select parameters depending on H
    alpha = pm.math.switch(H, alpha_pres, alpha_fail)
    beta  = pm.math.switch(H, beta_pres,  beta_fail)

    # Likelihood for observed fidelities
    pm.Beta("F_obs", alpha=alpha, beta=beta, observed=F)

    # -------------------------------
    # INFERENCE
    # -------------------------------
    trace = pm.sample(4000, tune=2000, target_accept=0.9)

# -------------------------------
# POSTERIOR ANALYSIS
# -------------------------------
az.summary(trace, var_names=["pi", "alpha_pres", "beta_pres", "alpha_fail", "beta_fail", "H"])

# Compute posterior probability P(H=1 | F)
H_samples = trace.posterior["H"].values.flatten()
posterior_prob_H1 = np.mean(H_samples)

- Hierarchical independent-Beta model across multiple experiments/components (pooled)

```python
# hierarchical_beta_model.py
import pymc as pm
import arviz as az
import numpy as np

def build_and_sample_hierarchical_beta(F_array,  # shape (J, n)
                                       draws=2000, tune=1500, chains=2,
                                       prior_settings=None):
    """
    F_array: numpy array (J, n) of observed fidelities in (0,1).
    Returns: PyMC trace, model
    """
    J, n = F_array.shape
    if prior_settings is None:
        prior_settings = dict(
            mu_pres_a=8, mu_pres_b=2,
            mu_fail_a=1, mu_fail_b=3,
            kappa_alpha=2, kappa_beta=0.5,
            pi_a=1, pi_b=1
        )
    with pm.Model() as model:
        # Global prior for proportion of preserved experiments
        pi = pm.Beta("pi", prior_settings["pi_a"], prior_settings["pi_b"])

        # Experiment-level latent H for each j
        H = pm.Bernoulli("H", p=pi, shape=J)

        # Group-level (per component i) priors for mu and kappa for H=1 and H=0
        mu_pres = pm.Beta("mu_pres", alpha=prior_settings["mu_pres_a"],
                                   beta=prior_settings["mu_pres_b"], shape=n)
        mu_fail = pm.Beta("mu_fail", alpha=prior_settings["mu_fail_a"],
                                   beta=prior_settings["mu_fail_b"], shape=n)
        kappa_pres = pm.Gamma("kappa_pres", alpha=prior_settings["kappa_alpha"],
                                         beta=prior_settings["kappa_beta"], shape=n)
        kappa_fail = pm.Gamma("kappa_fail", alpha=prior_settings["kappa_alpha"],
                                         beta=prior_settings["kappa_beta"], shape=n)

        alpha_pres = pm.Deterministic("alpha_pres", mu_pres * kappa_pres)
        beta_pres  = pm.Deterministic("beta_pres", (1 - mu_pres) * kappa_pres)
        alpha_fail = pm.Deterministic("alpha_fail", mu_fail * kappa_fail)
        beta_fail  = pm.Deterministic("beta_fail", (1 - mu_fail) * kappa_fail)

        # Select parameters for each experiment j depending on H[j]
        # We need shapes to broadcast: create (J,n) arrays
        alpha_pres_mat = pm.math.tile(alpha_pres, (J,1))  # (J,n)
        beta_pres_mat  = pm.math.tile(beta_pres,  (J,1))
        alpha_fail_mat = pm.math.tile(alpha_fail, (J,1))
        beta_fail_mat  = pm.math.tile(beta_fail,  (J,1))

        # choose row-wise by H
        alpha_mat = pm.math.switch(pm.math.reshape(H, (J,1)), alpha_pres_mat, alpha_fail_mat)
        beta_mat  = pm.math.switch(pm.math.reshape(H, (J,1)), beta_pres_mat,  beta_fail_mat)

        # Likelihood
        F_obs = pm.Beta("F_obs", alpha=alpha_mat, beta=beta_mat, observed=F_array)

        # Inference (NUTS)
        trace = pm.sample(draws=draws, tune=tune, chains=chains, target_accept=0.9)

    return model, trace
```

import numpy as np

def risk_aware_modulation(M, H, reward, value_est, kappa=0.5, eta=0.05):
    rpe = reward - value_est
    DA = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(rpe - 0.3)/0.1))
    risk = np.var([reward] * 10)  # Simplified variance
    T = 1.0 / (1 + kappa * abs(risk))  # Temperature
    # Modulated forward: sigmoid activation with modulation
    modulated = np.dot(M, H) + 0.1 * DA * H  # Simple element-wise
    H_prime = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-modulated / T))  # Soft sigmoid
    # Hebbian update (simplified discrete)
    tau = np.arange(-20, 21)
    K = np.exp(-np.abs(tau)/20) * np.sign(tau)
    hebb = eta * DA * np.sum(np.maximum(K, 0)) * np.outer(H_prime, H)  # Outer product approx
    M_updated = M + hebb[:M.shape[0], :M.shape[1]]  # Truncate to shape
    return H_prime, M_updated

def estimate_posterior_H_via_vi(F_array, prior_settings=None, vi_iter=20000):

#### 9.12.7 Hierarchical LogisticNormal Model (PyMC)  Verbatim Template

```python
# hierarchical_logistic_normal.py
import pymc as pm
import arviz as az
import numpy as np

def build_and_sample_hier_logistic_normal(F_array, draws=3000, tune=2000, chains=2,
                                          prior_settings=None):
    """
    F_array: numpy array (J, n) of fidelities in (0,1). Handles correlations via logistic-normal.
    Returns: PyMC model, trace
    """
    J, n = F_array.shape
    eps = 1e-6
    F_array = np.clip(F_array, eps, 1-eps)
    Y = np.log(F_array / (1 - F_array))  # logit transform

    if prior_settings is None:
        prior_settings = dict(
            pi_a=1, pi_b=1,
            mu0_pres=2.0, mu0_fail=-1.5,  # centers on logit scale
            mu0_sd=2.0,
            sd_scale=1.0, lkj_eta=2.0
        )

    with pm.Model() as model:
        # Hypothesis prior
        pi = pm.Beta("pi", prior_settings["pi_a"], prior_settings["pi_b"])
        # Discrete H per experiment (optional; see mixture marginalization below)
        # H = pm.Bernoulli("H", p=pi, shape=J)

        # Priors for means (logit space)
        mu0_pres = pm.Normal("mu0_pres", mu=prior_settings["mu0_pres"], sigma=prior_settings["mu0_sd"], shape=n)
        mu0_fail = pm.Normal("mu0_fail", mu=prior_settings["mu0_fail"], sigma=prior_settings["mu0_sd"], shape=n)

        # Covariance via Cholesky (SDs + LKJ correlation)
        chol_pres = pm.LKJCholeskyCov("chol_pres", n=n, eta=prior_settings["lkj_eta"], sd_dist=pm.HalfNormal.dist(prior_settings["sd_scale"]))
        L_pres = pm.expand_packed_triangular(n, chol_pres, lower=True)
        cov_pres = pm.Deterministic("cov_pres", L_pres @ L_pres.T)

        chol_fail = pm.LKJCholeskyCov("chol_fail", n=n, eta=prior_settings["lkj_eta"], sd_dist=pm.HalfNormal.dist(prior_settings["sd_scale"]))
        L_fail = pm.expand_packed_triangular(n, chol_fail, lower=True)
        cov_fail = pm.Deterministic("cov_fail", L_fail @ L_fail.T)

        # Mixture components (marginalize H for robustness)
        comp_pres = pm.MvNormal.dist(mu=mu0_pres, cov=cov_pres)
        comp_fail = pm.MvNormal.dist(mu=mu0_fail, cov=cov_fail)

        # Likelihood: mixture with weights [1-pi, pi]
        weights = pm.math.stack([1 - pi, pi])
        Y_obs = pm.Mixture("Y_obs", w=weights, comp_dists=[comp_fail, comp_pres], observed=Y)

        trace = pm.sample(draws=draws, tune=tune, chains=chains, target_accept=0.9)

    return model, trace
```

#### 9.12.10 Simulation Generators (Verbatim)

```python
# simulate_data.py
import numpy as np
from scipy.special import expit, logit

def simulate_hierarchical_beta(J=30, n=4, prop_pres=0.5,
                               mu_pres=0.9, mu_fail=0.2,
                               kappa_pres=50.0, kappa_fail=10.0,
                               rng=None):
    """
    Simulate J experiments; returns F_array (J,n) and true H vector.
    mu_pres / mu_fail can be scalars or arrays length n.
    kappa_* are precision parameters (higher -> lower variance).
    """
    rng = np.random.default_rng(rng)
    if np.isscalar(mu_pres):
        mu_pres = np.full(n, mu_pres)
    if np.isscalar(mu_fail):
        mu_fail = np.full(n, mu_fail)
    if np.isscalar(kappa_pres):
        kappa_pres = np.full(n, kappa_pres)
    if np.isscalar(kappa_fail):
        kappa_fail = np.full(n, kappa_fail)

    H = rng.binomial(1, prop_pres, size=J)
    F = np.zeros((J,n))
    for j in range(J):
        for i in range(n):
            if H[j]==1:
                a = mu_pres[i] * kappa_pres[i]
                b = (1-mu_pres[i]) * kappa_pres[i]
            else:
                a = mu_fail[i] * kappa_fail[i]
                b = (1-mu_fail[i]) * kappa_fail[i]
            F[j,i] = rng.beta(a, b)
    return F, H

def simulate_hierarchical_logistic_normal(J=30, n=4, prop_pres=0.5,
                                          mu_pres=None, mu_fail=None,
                                          cov_pres=None, cov_fail=None,
                                          rng=None):
    """
    Simulate logistic-normal hierarchical data: return F (J,n), H.
    mu_pres/mu_fail: vectors in logit space or probability-space; if in prob space transform.
    cov_*: covariance matrices in logit space.
    """
    rng = np.random.default_rng(rng)
    if mu_pres is None:
        mu_pres = np.full(n, logit(0.9))  # logit(0.9)
    else:
        if np.any((mu_pres>1) | (mu_pres<0)):
            pass
        else:
            mu_pres = logit(np.array(mu_pres))
    if mu_fail is None:
        mu_fail = np.full(n, logit(0.2))
    else:
        if np.any((mu_fail>1) | (mu_fail<0)):
            pass
        else:
            mu_fail = logit(np.array(mu_fail))
    if cov_pres is None:
        cov_pres = np.eye(n) * 0.5
    if cov_fail is None:
        cov_fail = np.eye(n) * 1.0

    H = rng.binomial(1, prop_pres, size=J)
    Y = np.zeros((J,n))
    for j in range(J):
        if H[j]==1:
            Y[j,:] = rng.multivariate_normal(mean=mu_pres, cov=cov_pres)
        else:
            Y[j,:] = rng.multivariate_normal(mean=mu_fail, cov=cov_fail)
    F = expit(Y)  # inverse logit
    eps = 1e-6
    F = np.clip(F, eps, 1-eps)
    return F, H
```


#### 9.12.11 Risk‑Aware Hebbian Update and Lyapunov Stability (Verbatim)

- Reward prediction error and DA activation:
  - r(t) = R(t) − V(t)
  - DA(t) = 1 / (1 + exp(−(r(t) − 0.3)/0.1))
- Risk and temperature modulation:
  - ρ(t) = Var[R | H(t)]
  - T(t) = T0 / (1 + κ·|ρ(t)|), κ = 0.5
- Hebbian update with STDP kernel:
  - K(τ) = exp(−|τ|/20) · sign(τ)  (20 ms window)
  - M(t+1) = M(t) + η ∫ [ DA(t)·max(K(τ),0) + D2_act·min(K(τ),0) ] · ( H(t) ⊗ H′(t) ) dτ,  η = 0.05
- Lyapunov correction (stability):
  - If ||dH/dt||₂ > 5 or Φ < 0.3, apply H(t+1) = H(t) − μ ∇L_stab
  - L_stab = ||H(t)||₂² + λ·||N(t)||₁

#### 9.12.12 Posterior Predictive Checks and Sensitivity (Verbatim)

- PPC:

#### 9.12.15 PPC & SBC Notebook Skeleton (Verbatim Excerpts)

- Posterior predictive checks (general):
  1. From posterior samples of parameters (and latent H when needed), draw replicated datasets F^rep from the model (use pm.sample_posterior_predictive).
  2. Compare observed summary statistics (component means, minimum, maximum, pairwise correlations, spectral overlap) to the distribution of those statistics under F^rep. If the observed statistics are extreme under the model, the model is mis-specified.
  3. Use graphical PPCs (histograms, scatter matrices) and numeric summaries (p-values of tail probabilities).
- Simulation-Based Calibration (SBC): verify MCMC sampling & inference by simulating parameters from priors, simulating F, re-inferring and checking rank statistics of true params — ensures inference code is well-behaved.
- SBC procedure (sketch):
  1. Choose a range of parameter values for μ_{i,1}, κ_{i,1} and μ_{i,0}, κ_{i,0} (pilot or plausible).
  2. For each draw: simulate F under H=1 and H=0 many times (e.g., 1000), fit model to each simulated F (or compute analytic posteriors if conjugate), compute P(H|F), and record proportion where P(H|F)> threshold.
  3. Summarize operating characteristics: true positive rate, false positive rate as a function of threshold and sample size. Use these to pick experiment scale & thresholds.

#### 9.12.16 Instrumentation & Signal Processing Details (Verbatim)

- Pump–probe heterodyne: preserves phase; necessary to reconstruct φ(ω). Use lock-in amplifiers and stabilized optical frequency combs for absolute phase stability.
- Stimulated Brillouin scattering: generate coherent phonons and measure their dispersion/phase across micron scales. Use high-Q waveguides and tapered fibers for coupling.
- NV center arrays: for magnetic/electric signatures localized to sub-cellular volumes; combine with machine-learning denoising to extract weak signals.
- 2D spectroscopy / echo sequences: to extract coherence times T2 and off-diagonal density matrix elements (indirectly).
- Cryo vs room-temp tradeoffs: many quantum coherences are fragile at physiological temperature; identify which modes persist at room temperature and focus on biologically realistic modes or engineer environmental protection (dynamic decoupling; structured bath engineering).
- NV magnetometry acquisition: acquire spectral sweeps and time traces synchronized to pump–probe cycles (if applicable) to look for correlated magnetic signatures. Use lock-in detection and dynamic decoupling sequences to suppress low-frequency noise and increase AC sensitivity.
- Denoising: apply wavelet denoising or model-based denoising; for NV signals use lock-in / matched filter approaches.
- Example acquisition parameters (starter): NV magnetometry — ensemble diamond (100 µm × 100 µm × 50 µm), CW ODMR with microwave drive; optical pumping 532 nm at < 500 µW/µm² to avoid heating; lock-in detection at kHz.

#### 9.12.21 QHRF Data Analysis Pipeline (Verbatim Excerpt)

- All raw data are stored in HDF5/NWB with full metadata (instrument settings, timestamps, sample ID, environmental logs). Analysis steps:
- Preprocessing
  1) Dark & background subtraction: remove instrument background using buffer-only scans.
  2) Deconvolution of instrument response: apply IRF deconvolution for time-domain pump–probe traces.
  3) Denoising: apply wavelet denoising or model-based denoising; for NV signals use lock-in / matched filter approaches.
- Spectral estimation
  4) PSD & cross-spectra: compute Welch’s PSD; compute cross-spectral density matrices S_ij(ω) for sensor arrays (Brillouin voxels, NV channels).
  5) Modal decomposition: spectral decomposition (SVD/eigen) of S(ω) to identify modes {ω_k} and shapes v_k. Fit Lorentzian/Fano lines to extract centers and linewidths Γ_k.
  6) Phase reconstruction: from heterodyne data or via analytic signal (Hilbert transform) reconstruct φ(ω). Compute A(ω)=|A|e^{iφ}.

2.0 The Foundational Calculus of HTLA: A Review of Linear Principles

Before detailing the nonlinear extensions, it is essential to establish a clear understanding of the existing Hypervector Linear Transdimensional Algebra (HTLA) framework that serves as the foundation. This review provides the vocabulary and theoretical context necessary to appreciate the profound shifts introduced by HTNLA.

2.1 Hypervector Spaces and Direct Sum Notation

The core data structure in HTLA is the hypervector, which is not a single, monolithic vector but a composite entity. A hypervector is formally defined as a tuple of vectors, H = (H_ℓ)_{ℓ ∈ L}, where each H_ℓ is a vector that resides in a distinct, labeled vector space ℝ^{n_ℓ}.1 This structure, represented by the direct sum notation,
H = ⨁_{ℓ∈L} ℝ^{n_ℓ}, is a deliberate and crucial design choice. Unlike simple vector concatenation, which would flatten the distinct identities of each component, the direct sum preserves the identity and dimensionality of each labeled component. This allows the system to treat multi-modal or multi-source information as a single algebraic entity without losing its inherent structural and semantic meaning.1

2.2 Transdimensional Operators as Block Matrices

Operations between hypervector spaces are mediated by Transdimensional Operators. A linear map, L: H_s → H_t, which transforms a source hypervector H_s into a target hypervector H_t, is defined as a block matrix, L = [L_{t,s}]_{t∈T,s∈S}.1 Each block within this matrix,
L_{t,s} ∈ ℝ^{q_t × p_s}, functions as a standard linear map, transforming a specific component from the source space (s) into a component in the target space (t). This block matrix formalism provides the mechanism for "seamless transitions and interactions" between components of varying dimensions, allowing for complex data transformations while respecting the underlying labeled structure.1

2.3 The Graded Inner Product and Label Kernels

To quantify the relationships and similarities within hypervector spaces, HTLA employs a Graded Inner Product, defined as ⟨H, K⟩_w = ∑_{ℓ∈L} w_ℓ ⟨H_ℓ, K_ℓ⟩.1 A key innovation within this formulation is the introduction of a
label kernel, w = {w_ℓ}_{ℓ∈L}. This is a set of weights assigned to each dimension label that allows the system to explicitly encode the semantic or contextual importance of different data modalities. This moves the system beyond a purely structural linear algebra into a framework that can integrate external knowledge, enabling a more nuanced and context-aware understanding of the relationships between hypervectors. For instance, in a multi-modal system, the weight assigned to an image label might be higher than that of a text label for a task where visual information is more critical.1

2.4 Core Theoretical Guarantees

The robustness of the HTLA framework is underpinned by a series of theoretical guarantees. The Concentration of Measure theorem is a cornerstone of this robustness, proving that for high-dimensional vectors with random components, the probability of their inner product deviating significantly from its mean is extremely low.1 This property is critical, as it ensures that small perturbations or noise in individual vector components do not drastically alter the overall inner product, which is a fundamental operation in similarity-based algorithms. Another key guarantee is the
Operator Convergence theorem. This theorem ensures that sequences of HTLA operators, such as those refined during iterative learning processes, will converge to stable solutions rather than diverging.1 This provides a solid theoretical basis for developing and analyzing learning algorithms within the HTLA framework, setting a crucial precedent for the stability analysis of the nonlinear learning rules introduced by HTNLA.

The Foundational Synthesis: HTNLA synthesizes the structured, multi-modal algebra of HTLA with the expressive power of non-linear neural networks. It provides a principled way to perform non-linear operations on labeled hypervectors, a key advantage over monolithic deep learning architectures that typically flatten and lose the semantic identity of data modalities.1
Non-linearity as Structured Evolution: In HTNLA, non-linearity is a targeted, structured mechanism, not a monolithic black box. The concept of Nonlinear Transdimensional Networks is central to this paradigm.1 In this model, non-linear
activation functions are applied at the component-vector level after a linear transformation. This allows for a multi-task architecture where non-linearities for a text modality can be distinct from those for an image modality. This structured non-linearity models the intricate, non-uniform cross-talk between different cognitive modules in a biologically plausible way.1
