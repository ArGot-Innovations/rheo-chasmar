Architectural Specification of a Multi-Module Computational Swarm Node
Introduction and High-Level Objective
The contemporary landscape of distributed systems and artificial intelligence is increasingly constrained by the limitations of centralized architectures, cloud-dependent orchestration, and monolithic state management paradigms. As computational demands scale, the necessity for robust, decentralized, and highly resilient network topologies becomes paramount. Inspired by the functional segregation observed in complex multi-phase systems, this research report delineates a comprehensive, simulation-ready specification for a non-biological Multi-Module Computational Node architecture. The fundamental objective is to design, formalize, and
evaluate a decentralized swarm of nodes—with a primary target operational scale of
—capable of emergent multi-scale consensus and highly resilient salience propagation.1
This architecture systematically strips away centralized coordination mechanisms, operating entirely under extreme per-node constraints. Each computational unit is strictly limited to exactly one 64-bit packet emission per micro-cycle and an ingress capacity hard-capped at eight 64-bit packets.3 Global state variables, synchronized global clocks, and unbounded memory architectures are explicitly forbidden. Instead, nodes possess asynchronous local
clocks with a bounded drift of , and rely on highly localized, mathematically rigorous interactions to establish macro-level coherence. The functional equivalent of a tri-compartment design is realized purely through computational primitives: an Identity module (Core-A), a Prediction module (Core-B), and a Regulation module (Core-C), all governed by a central Processing Layer and an Interface Layer.5
A critical mandate of this architecture is its capacity to endure and autonomously recover from catastrophic structural lesions, specifically a sudden 30% uniform random node deletion. To satisfy this requirement, the architecture integrates Hypervector Transdimensional Linear Algebra (HTLA) for dense state encoding and relies on a localized, Lyapunov-style stability heuristic to dynamically govern topology and consensus thresholds.8 This document exhaustively details the mathematical formulations, bit-level protocols, data structures, computational limits, and empirical evaluation frameworks required to implement and validate this swarm architecture.
Architectural Specification
The internal architecture of the computational node is rigidly partitioned to ensure that localized operational faults or adversarial data injections do not precipitate a catastrophic collapse of the node's entire state vector. This segregation ensures parallel execution viability within the tightly bounded micro-cycle and maintains a strict separation of concerns among cryptographic identity, state prediction, and localized thermodynamic control.
Core-A: Identity Module
Core-A serves as the immutable cryptographic and hyperdimensional anchor of the computational node. In traditional networked systems, identity is managed via central registries or extended handshake protocols, both of which are strictly prohibited under the 64-bit bandwidth constraint. Therefore, Core-A generates, maintains, and verifies the node's unique spatial-temporal signature using a high-dimensional base vector generated at initialization. This module utilizes Hypervector Transdimensional Linear Algebra (HTLA) to project compact 12-bit
physical identifiers into a vast, sparse mathematical space—typically dimensions—where randomly generated vectors are inherently nearly orthogonal.8
The primary function of Core-A is to evaluate the structural integrity and origin authenticity of incoming packets. Because the Interface Layer drops any packets beyond the first eight received in a cycle, Core-A must maintain a localized, highly efficient neighbor cache. It calculates the Hamming distance or cosine similarity between the incoming hypervector slices and the locally cached history of known neighbors. If an incoming packet claims an identity that mathematically conflicts with the expected cyclic permutation of its HTLA slice, Core-A instantly flags the payload as a Byzantine or collision-corrupted artifact, instructing the Processing Layer to discard it. The fundamental assumption here is that malicious actors cannot successfully guess the temporal permutation of a 10,000-bit vector utilizing only a 16-bit observable slice per cycle, thus securing the local topology against Sybil and spoofing attacks.1
Core-B: Prediction Module
Core-B functions as the node's forward-looking state estimator. Operating entirely in isolation from network I/O, it utilizes an internal Markovian transition matrix applied to a sliding temporal window of historical salience states. The module continuously projects the expected state of the local swarm neighborhood for the upcoming cycle. Upon receiving the validated ingress data from the Processing Layer, Core-B calculates the delta between its internal prediction and the observed reality.
This calculated delta generates a highly sensitive local error signal. The rationale for this module stems from the need to prevent the swarm from oscillating wildly in response to transient noise. If the local error signal remains within acceptable statistical bounds, the node implicitly trusts its environment. However, if the delta exceeds a dynamic threshold—indicative of a fundamental phase shift in the environment or a structural network failure—Core-B triggers Core-C. A limitation of this module is its susceptibility to over-fitting in highly static environments; to counteract this, a deterministic thermal noise variable is injected into the
prediction matrix, ensuring the module remains continuously reactive.12
Core-C: Regulation Module
Core-C operates as the localized control heuristic engine, fundamentally governing the internal thermodynamics and behavior of the node. It receives the error delta from Core-B and applies a distributed Lyapunov drift-plus-penalty framework.9 Core-C dynamically adjusts critical operational parameters, including the salience weight of the node's next broadcast, the internal consensus threshold, and the logical boundaries of the local connectivity graph.
When Core-B detects an anomaly, or when the Interface Layer registers a sudden, sustained drop in incoming packet frequency—a primary indicator of a physical network lesion—Core-C executes localized recovery heuristics. It effectively acts as an autonomic nervous system analogue, suppressing packet storms by raising thresholds during high-density operations, and conversely lowering thresholds and emitting "Lesion Alert" flags to encourage aggressive topological rewiring during crisis states.14
Processing and Interface Layers
The Processing Layer acts as the central synchronous bus, bridging the asynchronous external environment with the deterministic internal cores. It is responsible for enforcing the micro-cycle update schedule, executing all Arithmetic Logic Unit (ALU) operations, and strictly maintaining the precise 256-byte State Vector. It handles the bit-shifting and masking operations necessary to unpack the 64-bit payloads before distributing the data to the respective cores.
The Interface Layer is the purely mechanical execution boundary of the node. It simulates the physical transmission and reception of data. To enforce the non-biological constraints, the Interface Layer contains a rigid ingress buffer. In a high-density swarm, a node's spatial radius
may encompass dozens of peers. The Interface Layer employs a stochastic reception protocol, generally favoring signals with higher simulated physical amplitude, to load exactly eight 64-bit packets into the buffer. Any packet arriving at the interface after the eighth slot within a single local clock cycle is deterministically and permanently dropped.3 This mechanism naturally enforces communication sparsity, forcing the network to rely on recursive grouping and multi-hop diffusion rather than fully connected cliques.
Inter-Module Coupling Graph and Deterministic Schedule
To ensure absolute reproducibility, the internal execution of every node follows a deterministic, 7-tick micro-cycle schedule driven entirely by the node's local clock, which is subject to a
natural asynchronous drift relative to the global observer.
The micro-cycle begins at Tick 0, where the Interface Layer locks the ingress buffer, making up to eight 64-bit packets available to the Processing Layer. During Tick 1, the Processing Layer unpacks these payloads and verifies the Cyclic Redundancy Check (CRC). Packets failing the
CRC are immediately purged to conserve subsequent compute cycles. At Tick 2, Core-A takes over, projecting the sender IDs into HTLA space and calculating distances against the neighbor cache to detect Byzantine behavior. Tick 3 involves Core-B computing the expected state vector and calculating the prediction error delta against the valid incoming payloads.
At Tick 4, Core-C utilizes the prediction error and the current Lyapunov drift metric to calculate the required internal state adjustments, subsequently setting the outgoing salience multiplier and the header flags. Tick 5 is the synthesis phase; the Processing Layer executes the mathematical state update equations, binds the updated salience and identity into an outgoing hypervector slice, and finalizes the internal variables. Finally, at Tick 6, the Interface Layer receives the newly constructed 64-bit packet and broadcasts it to the spatial neighborhood, concluding the cycle.

Emission Rules, Heartbeats, and Collision Handling
A node is bound by a strict physical law within the simulation: it must emit exactly one packet per micro-cycle. If a node has processed no new information and its internal salience remains static, it is required to emit a "heartbeat" packet. This packet contains its standard ID and HTLA slice but carries a null payload flag. Heartbeats are critical; they supply the necessary energy to maintain the topological coupling graph, allowing neighboring Core-A modules to keep their
connection weights from decaying to zero.
The collision handling mechanics are dictated by the 8-packet ingress limit. In regions of high
swarm density, the physical transmission radius results in significant spatial overlap. The Interface Layer mitigates this by applying a stochastic filter analogous to inverse-square signal degradation. The first eight preamble-synchronized packets that successfully clear the hardware CRC-16 checksum are written to the ingress buffer. All subsequent arrivals in that cycle suffer simulated physical collision and are discarded without processing.3 This mechanism forces the swarm to act as a sparse, dynamically shifting graph rather than a monolithic block, drastically improving resilience against localized jamming.

Strict Computational Limits
To enforce the non-biological, low-compute premise, the simulation tracks every operation. A node that exceeds its budget triggers a simulated hardware fault and goes offline.
Integer Operations: Hard-capped at 120 per micro-cycle. This covers all bit shifts, masking, array index offsets, and CRC calculations.
Floating-Point Operations: Hard-capped at 45 per micro-cycle. Reserved strictly for the salience diffusion weighting and Core-B's prediction delta calculations.
Hypervector Operations: Limited to 16 XOR instructions and 1 memory permutation per cycle.
Forbidden Operations: Nodes are strictly prohibited from performing dynamic memory allocation (no malloc or equivalent instantiations during the active run loop). Nodes may not query global spatial coordinates; physical distance must be exclusively inferred by statistical packet dropout rates. Furthermore, nodes are mathematically barred from executing global reduction operations to find true swarm averages.

Failure Modes and Ablation Experiments
To rigorously validate the necessity of the tri-module design and the strict operational constraints, the architecture is subjected to exhaustive failure analysis and ablation testing. This isolates the protective mechanisms and demonstrates the precise theoretical cause of catastrophic failure when elements are removed.
Enumeration of Meaningful Failure Modes
The architecture is theoretically vulnerable to specific failure states if the internal heuristics are overwhelmed:
1. Interface Ingress Saturation (Packet Storms): In highly dense physical environments, local clusters may generate traffic that vastly exceeds the 8-packet buffer limit. Without stochastic physical dropouts, a node might systematically drop packets from a specific physical vector, creating a blind spot. The architecture mitigates this via randomized physical-layer reception scheduling, ensuring statistical fairness over a temporal window.
2. Core-A Cache Poisoning: A highly coordinated Byzantine actor might attempt to rapidly cycle its 12-bit Node ID, flooding the victim's 8-slot neighbor_cache with phantom identities and forcing out legitimate, stabilizing peers. The architecture mitigates this by
requiring temporal stability; new IDs are quarantined with a near-zero coupling weight until they have successfully transmitted at least 20 mathematically consistent HTLA permutations.
3. Core-B Over-Fitting (Mathematical Freezing): In exceptionally static environments
where the salience does not change, Core-B's prediction error approaches exactly zero. If the Lyapunov energy drops too low, Core-C may raise the consensus threshold so high that the node becomes mathematically rigid, ignoring legitimate phase shifts. The
continuous injection of thermal noise guarantees a minimum baseline drift, preventing localized permanent freezing.12
