### 3. Is the proposed law `imaginable ≠ probable ≠ believed ≠ authoritative` architecturally sound? Attack it before accepting it.

**The law is sound as a *normative principle*. It is dangerously underspecified as an *architectural constraint*.**

**Attack 1: What does "≠" mean computationally?**

In a neural architecture, these four categories are not neatly separated. They are continuous distributions over the same representational space. `imaginable` and `probable` may share the same generative pathways; the difference is a *threshold*, not a *type*. If the threshold is implemented as a scalar parameter, a sufficiently persistent green-sky hypothesis could gradually cross it through repeated generation—especially over 10,000 years. The law says "≠" but the architecture needs to enforce *non-communicating compartments* or *asymmetric update rules* to make it true.

**Attack 2: The law privileges generation over promotion, but says nothing about *retraction*.**

What happens when a once-promoted belief (sky=blue, for 5,000 years) is *demoted*? The law only describes the flow from imagination to authority. It does not describe the reverse flow—how an authoritative belief is *unlearned*, *revised*, or *deprecated* when reality changes. A 10,000-year mind needs a robust *retraction* mechanism, not just a conservative promotion gate. Without it, the system accumulates *zombie beliefs*—once-true propositions that linger even after they cease to be true.

**Attack 3: The law is silent on *intermediate* states.**

What about beliefs that are *entertained* but not *promoted*—hypotheses that are kept "in reserve" as live possibilities? Over millennia, the system may accumulate thousands of such hypotheses. How are they maintained? How are they *reweighted* as new evidence arrives? The law gives us two gates but no theory of the *space between* them.

**Attack 4: "Authoritative" is ambiguous.**

Does "authoritative" mean "believed by the system" or "acted upon by the system"? These are different. A system might believe sky=green (based on sensor data) but *act as if* sky=blue (because acting on green would be catastrophic). The law collapses this distinction.

### 5. How should Nexus preserve epistemic history—not merely factual history—over millennia?

**Epistemic history requires a *hypergraph* of belief trajectories, not a linear timeline.**

Factual history is a sequence of states:

```
sky=blue (t0→t1)
sky=green (t1→t2)
sky=blue (t2→t3)
```

Epistemic history is a *tree* or *hypergraph* of *what the system believed, when, why, and with what confidence*:

```
At t0: belief(sky=blue, confidence=0.95, source=observation, alternatives={sky=blue:0.95, sky=green:0.01, ...})
At t1: observation reports green. Belief(sky=blue, confidence=0.60). Alternatives: {sensor_error:0.20, atmospheric_change:0.15, ...}
At t2: multiple observations confirm green. Belief(sky=green, confidence=0.85). Revision triggered by: corroboration.
At t3: reversion to blue. Belief(sky=blue, confidence=0.92). Historical note: "this happened once before, 300 years ago."
```

**Architectural implications:**

1. **Every belief needs a provenance record**—not just "source" but *evidential chain*.
2. **Confidence should be a trajectory, not a scalar**—the system should remember *how confidence evolved*.
3. **Alternatives must be retained**—the system should remember *what it considered* and *why it rejected* each alternative.
4. **Revision triggers must be logged**—the system should know *why* it changed its mind.
5. **Historical beliefs should be queryable**—the system should be able to answer "What did you believe on the first day the sky appeared green?"

This is not solved by a fact database. It requires a *belief hypergraph* with temporal and epistemic dimensions.

**Critical open question:** How does the system *consolidate* epistemic history without unbounded storage growth? Some form of *lossy compression* with *importance-weighted retention* is needed—but the importance metric must itself be historical (what was important *then* may differ from what is important *now*).

### 7. What would true ontology extension require beyond ordinary compositional generation?

**Ontology extension is not "new combinations of old parts." It is the introduction of *new primitives*.**

Compositional generation takes existing concepts and recombines them: "green sky" = green + sky. That is not ontology extension—it is *combinatorial play* within an existing ontology.

**True ontology extension** is:

```
Ω_{t+1} = Ω_t ∪ {c_new}
```

where `c_new` is a *primitive* that **cannot be defined** in terms of existing primitives.

Examples:
- The concept of **"quantum field"** could not be defined in terms of Newtonian particles.
- The concept of **"gene"** could not be defined in terms of Mendelian traits.
- The concept of **"algorithm"** could not be defined in terms of arithmetic operations.

**What this requires architecturally:**

1. **A mechanism for proposing new primitives**—not just new combinations.
2. **A coherence test**—does the new primitive *make sense* with existing ontology?
3. **A utility test**—does the new primitive *explain* something that was previously inexplicable?
4. **A fallback**—if the new primitive is rejected, it must not contaminate the ontology.
5. **A consolidation mechanism**—if accepted, it must be *integrated* with existing concepts, not merely appended.

**The hard problem:** How does a system *generate* a genuinely new primitive? This is the **"conceptual innovation"** problem. Noise + perturbation does not solve it. Some form of **analogical mapping** from other domains, combined with **abstraction** and **inference to the best explanation**, is needed.

**Critical challenge:** How do you test a new primitive without already having the concepts needed to understand it? This is the **"bootstrapping"** problem of ontology extension.

### 8. How can Nexus remain open to unprecedented reality without becoming gullible?

**The key insight:** Gullibility is not a failure of generation—it is a failure of *integration* and *actuation*. A system can generate and entertain *any* hypothesis without being gullible, as long as it does not *integrate* or *act on* that hypothesis without sufficient evidence.

**The solution may be a *two-track* system:**

- **Track 1 (Operational model):** The system's *working* model of reality. Updated conservatively.
- **Track 2 (Exploratory model):** A set of *alternative* models. Updated permissively.

When evidence for an alternative model becomes sufficiently strong, it is *promoted* to the operational model. This is essentially a **hypothesis testing** framework—but with the critical feature that **alternative models are retained even when not promoted**, so the system can *revisit* them later.

## The Cap Problem: A Final Adversarial Note

The prompt uses `cap` as a "fantastic test" because no cosmic catastrophe is required—just ordinary linguistic evolution.

**But this actually exposes a deeper problem:** If `cap` can drift from "covering" to "lie" in a few decades, how many *other* symbols will drift over 10,000 years? Thousands? Millions?

**The system cannot maintain a separate epistemic history for every symbol.** Some form of *compression* and *generalization* is needed.

**The key insight:** Semantic drift is not random. It follows *patterns*—metaphor, metonymy, generalization, specialization, euphemism, dysphemism. A 10,000-year mind needs to *learn these patterns* and use them to *predict* future drift, not just *record* past drift.

This implies that **epistemic history is not just about *remembering*—it is about *understanding the dynamics of change*.** The system needs a *theory of semantic evolution*, not just a *database of semantic facts*.

**That is a much harder problem than the prompt acknowledges.** But it is the right problem.

## 2. What does a 10,000-year cognitive individual require that present-day AI architecture generally does not?

## 3. Is the proposed law **imaginable ≠ probable ≠ believed ≠ authoritative** architecturally sound? Attack it before accepting it.

**It is sound as a conceptual distinction, but dangerously incomplete as an architectural specification.**

### Attack 1: The chain is not a total order

The law implies a linear progression: imagination → probability assessment → belief → authority. But real cognition is not linear. Consider:

- A hypothesis can be **believed but not imaginable** (e.g., quantum entanglement — physicists believe it, many cannot truly imagine it)
- A hypothesis can be **authoritative but not believed** (e.g., a constitutional constraint that the system treats as binding despite personal skepticism — relevant for HIGC)
- A hypothesis can be **probable but not imaginable** (e.g., a mathematical theorem whose proof is probabilistically correct but whose content defies intuition)

The chain `imaginable → probable → believed → authoritative` embeds an implicit cognitive pipeline that may not match actual cognitive dynamics. A better formulation might be a **partially ordered lattice** or a **directed acyclic graph** of epistemic states, not a chain.

### Attack 2: "Imaginable" is not a binary gate

The prompt defines `G_generate(h)` as a binary decision: may hypothesis *h* be entertained? But imagination is not binary. Consider:

- *h₁*: "The sky is green" (counterfactual, easily entertained)
- *h₂*: "The sky is conscious and experiences qualia" (metaphysically controversial, but constructible)
- *h₃*: "The sky is a 7-dimensional manifold intersecting our 3D space" (requires ontology extension)
- *h₄*: "The sky is [concept that cannot be expressed in my current ontology]" (genuinely unimaginable)

`G_generate` must be **graded**, not binary. It needs to distinguish:
- Readily imaginable (within current ontology, low cognitive cost)
- Imaginable with effort (requires temporary ontology relaxation)
- Imaginable only with ontology extension (requires introducing new conceptual primitives)
- Currently unimaginable (no path exists from current ontology)

If `G_generate` is binary and permissive, it collapses these distinctions. If it is binary and restrictive, it blocks ontology extension.

### Attack 3: "Probable" is ambiguous between epistemic and subjective probability

`probable` in the chain could mean:
- P(h | current evidence, current ontology) — epistemic probability
- P(h | current evidence, current ontology, current cognitive state) — includes biases, attention, etc.
- P(h | all possible evidence, current ontology) — counterfactual probability

These are not the same. A system that conflates them will make systematic errors. For example, "low prior probability given current evidence" is not the same as "low probability in principle." The Green Sky problem specifically requires preserving the distinction: the sky turning green has low *epistemic* probability at *t₀* but may have high *actual* probability at *t₁*.

### Attack 4: "Authoritative" conflates epistemic and normative authority

The prompt notes that CA should not relax "constitutional law" or "identity protection" even when imagination is permissive. But this means `authoritative` is doing two jobs:
1. Epistemic authority: "This is what I believe the world to be"
2. Normative authority: "This is what I am constitutionally bound to treat as true"

These can conflict. A system might have high epistemic confidence that a constitutional constraint is suboptimal, while still treating it as normatively authoritative. The single `G_promote` gate cannot capture this duality without additional structure.

### Attack 5: The gates don't capture *reversibility*

A hypothesis can be promoted to authoritative, then *demoted*. The two-gate model as stated is acyclic. But epistemic history requires that demotion be tracked as a *trajectory*, not merely a state change. "I used to believe X, now I believe Y" is not the same as "I believe Y, and X is false." The former preserves epistemic history; the latter destroys it.

### Revised formulation

Instead of a chain, consider a **state machine on a partially ordered set**:

```
States: {unimaginable, imaginable, entertained, probable, believed, authoritative, superseded, retracted}

Transitions:
  unimaginable → imaginable (ontology extension)
  imaginable → entertained (G_generate permits)
  entertained → probable (evidence accumulation)
  entertained → unimaginable (evidence refutes possibility)
  probable → believed (confidence threshold)
  probable → entertained (evidence weakens)
  believed → authoritative (promotion criteria met)
  believed → probable (evidence weakens)
  authoritative → superseded (new evidence, new hypothesis promoted)
  authoritative → retracted (error correction)
  superseded → ... (preserved in epistemic history)
  retracted → ... (preserved in epistemic history)
```

The key insight: **superseded and retracted are terminal states that preserve history**, not merely transitions back to earlier states.

## 5. How should Nexus preserve epistemic history — not merely factual history — over millennia?

**This is the hardest problem in the entire prompt, and I do not think current Nexus architecture has a complete answer.**

### What epistemic history actually is

Factual history: "The sky was blue at t₀, green at t₁, blue at t₂."

Epistemic history:
- "At t₀, I believed the sky was blue with confidence 0.999, based on [evidence chain E₀]. I had no alternative hypotheses under consideration."
- "At t₁, I initially believed the green observation was a sensor malfunction (confidence 0.7). Over the next 10 years, as [evidence chain E₁] accumulated, my confidence in the sensor-malf hypothesis dropped to 0.1, and my confidence in the atmospheric-change hypothesis rose to 0.85. - "At t₂, I reverted to blue with confidence 0.999, based on [evidence chain E₂]. But I now know that my previous 0.999 confidence was *wrong*, which means my confidence calibration at t₀ was imperfect. I have updated my meta-confidence in my own high-confidence beliefs."

This is not a knowledge graph. It is a **narrative of belief revision** with self-referential meta-cognition.

### Why current architectures fail

**Vector databases:** Store embeddings, not belief trajectories. A single embedding for "sky" at each time step destroys the *reasoning* that led to the embedding.

**Knowledge graphs:** Store triples, not confidence trajectories. "sky color blue" is a fact, not a belief state.

**RAG:** Retrieves relevant documents, not the system's own past reasoning. It solves "what information was available?" not "how did I reason about that information?"

**Continual learning:** Preserves capability, not epistemic history. EWC prevents forgetting but does not preserve *why* weights were updated.

### Proposed architectural requirements

**a. Epistemic provenance as a first-class object**

Every belief in the authoritative world model must carry:
- The hypothesis that was entertained
- The evidence that supported/discounted it
- The reasoning chain that led to promotion
- The alternative hypotheses that were considered and rejected
- The confidence trajectory over time
- The cognitive regime under which the belief was formed

This is not metadata. It is **structural** — the belief is inseparable from its epistemic history.

**b. Temporal stratification of the world model**

The world model should not be a single "current best guess." It should be a **temporal stack** of epoch-specific world models, each tagged with:
- Temporal validity interval
- Ontology version
- Confidence calibration
- Meta-cognitive state

A query at time *t* projects the relevant epoch(s) based on the query's temporal reference. "What color is the sky?" → current epoch. "What color was the sky during the atmospheric event?" → epoch at t₁. "What did you believe on the first day the sky appeared green?" → the epistemic state at the *transition* between epochs.

**c. Hysteresis in belief revision**

The system must resist both:
- **Overwriting:** New belief destroys old belief
- **Averaging:** Multiple beliefs are collapsed into a summary statistic

This requires that belief updates be **additive, not replacement**. The new belief is *appended* to the epistemic history, not substituted for the old one. The "current" belief is a *projection* of the history, not the history itself.

**d. Meta-cognitive tracking**

The system must track:
- Its own confidence calibration (how often are my 0.99-confidence beliefs actually correct?)
- Its own reasoning biases (do I over-weight recent evidence?)
- Its own ontology evolution (what concepts have I added/removed over time?)

This is not merely "self-awareness." It is **epistemic self-modeling** — the system maintains a model of its own epistemic reliability, which is itself subject to the same temporal stratification.

**e. The "epistemic differential" representation**

Instead of storing beliefs as static propositions, store them as **differential equations** over time:

```
d(Belief)/dt = Evidence_inflow - Evidence_decay + Confidence_update - Ontology_shift
```

This is abstract, but the intuition is: a belief is not a point in belief space, but a **trajectory**. The "current" belief is the integral of this differential from initialization to now.

Where:
- `w` = symbol
- `t` = time
- `c` = context (community, register, etc.)
- `R(t)` = the set of possible relationships at time t (this itself may evolve)
- `C(t)` = the set of concepts at time t

The "meaning" of `cap` is not a point. It is a **trajectory through this manifold** over time, with branching (polysemy) and merging (semantic bleaching).

## 7. What would true ontology extension require beyond ordinary compositional generation?

**This is where the prompt's distinction between "surface novelty" and "structural novelty" becomes critical. True ontology extension is not merely combining existing concepts in new ways — it is introducing concepts that the previous ontology could not express.**

### Levels of novelty (revisited)

The prompt identifies:
1. Surface novelty (new token)
2. Compositional novelty (new combination)
3. Semantic reassignment (old symbol → new meaning)
4. Conceptual novelty (new category)
5. Structural novelty (new relation/operator)
6. Theory construction (new objects + rules + consequences)

True ontology extension requires levels 4–6. Levels 1–3 are operations *within* an existing ontology.

### What ontology extension actually requires

**a. Recognition of ontological incompleteness**

The system must be able to represent: "My current ontology cannot express phenomenon P." This is not the same as "P is anomalous" or "P has low probability." It is "P is *inexpressible* in my current conceptual framework."

This requires a **meta-ontological layer** — a representation of what the current ontology *can* and *cannot* express. This is not merely a list of known unknowns. It is a **structural characterization** of the ontology's expressive limits.

**b. Candidate ontology extension**

Given a recognition of incompleteness, the system must generate candidate extensions. This is not random perturbation. It is **structured hypothesis generation** about what kind of new concept/operator/relation would resolve the incompleteness.

For example, faced with the green sky anomaly:
- The current ontology has concepts: sky, color, atmospheric composition, light scattering
- The anomaly suggests: "None of these concepts, combined in any way I can construct, explain the observation."
- Ontology extension: "Perhaps there is a new kind of interaction between atmospheric particles and light that is not captured by Rayleigh scattering. I will hypothesize a new interaction type, `X-scattering`, with properties [specification]."

**c. Coherence testing of extended ontology**

The new concept must be tested for:
- Internal coherence (does it contradict itself?)
- External coherence (does it contradict well-established beliefs?)
- Explanatory power (does it resolve the anomaly?)
- Predictive power (does it generate testable predictions?)
- Parsimony (is it simpler than alternative extensions?)

**d. Conservative adoption**

Even if an extended ontology passes all tests, it should not immediately replace the old one. The system should maintain **multiple ontologies** and select the appropriate one based on context. The new ontology is a **candidate**, not a **replacement**, until it has demonstrated superior performance over a significant period.

### Why this is not "turning temperature up"

Temperature increase in LLMs increases the *variance* of sampled tokens. It does not:
- Introduce new conceptual primitives

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

def simulate(T, p_switch, p_correct, n_signals=3, ema_alpha=0.15, hyst_hi=0.62, hyst_lo=0.38):
    """
    True regime R_t in {0,1} with self-persistence controlled by p_switch
    (p_switch=0.01 -> slow-changing 'real' regime, dwell time ~100 steps;
     p_switch=0.5  -> memoryless ground truth, no real persistence at all).

    Each step emits n_signals independent noisy readings correlated with R_t
    (p_correct = chance any single reading matches the true regime) --
    these stand in for 'task type / uncertainty / resource budget' read fresh
    each cycle, exactly what the stateless hypothesis is allowed to use.

    STATELESS: majority vote of the n_signals at time t only. No memory.
    STATEFUL:  EMA of the signal average, with hysteresis (Schmitt trigger) --
               this is literally the 'dwell-time / deadband' mechanism THDE's
               own spec already claims to provide.
    """
    R = np.zeros(T, dtype=int)
    R[0] = rng.integers(0, 2)
    for t in range(1, T):
        R[t] = 1 - R[t-1] if rng.random() < p_switch else R[t-1]

    signals = np.zeros((T, n_signals), dtype=int)
    for t in range(T):
        for i in range(n_signals):
            correct = rng.random() < p_correct
            signals[t, i] = R[t] if correct else 1 - R[t]

    # Stateless: instantaneous majority vote, no memory across steps
    stateless = (signals.mean(axis=1) > 0.5).astype(int)

    # Stateful: EMA + hysteresis band (needs sustained evidence to flip)
    stateful = np.zeros(T, dtype=int)
    m = 0.5
    cur = R[0]
    for t in range(T):
        evidence = signals[t].mean()
        m = (1 - ema_alpha) * m + ema_alpha * evidence
        if cur == 0 and m > hyst_hi:
            cur = 1
        elif cur == 1 and m < hyst_lo:
            cur = 0
        stateful[t] = cur

    acc_stateless = (stateless == R).mean()
    acc_stateful  = (stateful  == R).mean()
    switches_true      = int(np.sum(R[1:] != R[:-1]))
    switches_stateless = int(np.sum(stateless[1:] != stateless[:-1]))
    switches_stateful  = int(np.sum(stateful[1:]  != stateful[:-1]))
    return acc_stateless, acc_stateful, switches_true, switches_stateless, switches_stateful

def run_condition(label, p_switch, n_trials=200, T=2000, p_correct=0.70):
    rows = [simulate(T, p_switch, p_correct) for _ in range(n_trials)]
    rows = np.array(rows, dtype=float)
    acc_sl, acc_sf, sw_true, sw_sl, sw_sf = rows.mean(axis=0)
    acc_sl_std, acc_sf_std = rows[:,0].std(), rows[:,1].std()
    print(f"--- {label} (p_switch={p_switch}, true dwell time ~{1/p_switch:.0f} steps) ---")
    print(f"  accuracy   stateless: {acc_sl:.3f} (+/-{acc_sl_std:.3f})   stateful: {acc_sf:.3f} (+/-{acc_sf_std:.3f})")
    print(f"  regime switches per {T} steps -> true: {sw_true:.1f}   stateless: {sw_sl:.1f}   stateful: {sw_sf:.1f}")
    print()

run_condition("CONDITION A: real regime is slow-changing", p_switch=0.01)
run_condition("CONDITION B: real regime has NO persistence at all", p_switch=0.5)
```

The technique, piece by piece:

**Ground truth (`R`).** A two-state process where the regime flips with probability `p_switch` each step and otherwise holds. This one parameter is the whole experiment: `p_switch=0.01` gives a mean dwell time of ~100 steps (a "real regime persists" world); `p_switch=0.5` makes each step's regime independent of the last (a "no persistence exists" world). Everything downstream is identical between conditions — only this assumption about the environment changes.

**Observable signals.** At every step, `n_signals=3` independent noisy readings are generated, each correct with probability `p_correct=0.70`. These stand in for "task type / uncertainty / resource budget read fresh each cycle" — the exact inputs Q12 said a stateless recomputation would have access to. Making them independent and noisy (not perfectly reliable) is what makes this a real test rather than a rigged one: if the raw signals were noiseless, of course a stateless reading would be perfect.

**Stateless competitor.** `signals.mean(axis=1) > 0.5` — majority vote across the three readings, computed fresh at every step with zero access to `t-1`. This is a fair implementation of "regime inferred fresh each cycle with no persistent state," not a strawman — it already benefits from fusing three independent signals, which is a real variance-reduction technique on its own.

**Stateful competitor.** An exponential moving average (`m`) of the per-step signal average, decayed via `ema_alpha=0.15`, feeding a Schmitt trigger: switch from 0→1 only once `m` climbs above `0.62`, switch back only once it falls below `0.38`. That gap between the two thresholds (the "hysteresis band") is what makes this cost something to flip — a handful of noisy readings in the other direction won't do it, sustained evidence will. This is a standard control-theory technique for exactly the chatter problem THDE's own spec names.

**Why 200 trials, not one.** A single run of a stochastic simulation can look good or bad by luck. Averaging accuracy and switch-count over 200 independently-seeded 2000-step runs per condition, and reporting the standard deviation alongside the mean, is what turns "I ran it once and it worked" into a number you can actually trust.

**Why two conditions instead of one.** Running only Condition A would have let the result look like a universal argument for persistent state. Running B too is what makes the finding honest: it shows the win is conditional on the environment actually having temporal structure, not an intrinsic property of stateful mechanisms being "better." That's the whole reason the write-up landed on "conditional, and testable" rather than a flat yes.

## 3. Attacking `imaginable ≠ probable ≠ believed ≠ authoritative`

**It forbids nothing.** Any four things can be declared distinct by defining them as distinct. A law has to rule out a system state.

$$\text{Authoritative} \subseteq \text{Believed} \subseteq \text{Plausible} \subseteq \text{Imaginable}$$

The architectural content is not the distinctness. ## 2. Dimension cost accounting

The bill is in Drive, split across the N-Dimensional Cognitive Engines spec. It's a different shape than I assumed.

**What does NOT grow:** HSL is fixed. d=1024, 36 primitives, 36·37/2 = 666 couplings, 30% activity cap (307 active). Quinary, 3 bit-planes. Fixed at birth. The HCPM routing table is a fixed bijective 36-permutation. None of that inflates over time.

**What grows:** the HTLA label set $L(t)$. The spec calls it "Hardware Remapping / Dynamic Dimension Adaptation — time-varying labels $L(T)$" and gives the performance target as **"linear scaling of capacity with new labels."** That's the only costing statement I found, and it's costing the wrong object.

The state is $H(t) = \bigoplus_{\ell \in L(t)} \mathbb{R}^{n_\ell}$ — linear in $|L|$, as advertised. But the operators are block matrices $L = [L_{t,s}]$, one block per ordered label pair. That grid is **quadratic in $|L|$**, and the operator grid is the real bill, not the state vector.

Concretely, at 10 new labels/year over 10⁴ years:

| Object | Size |
|---|---|
| Labels $\|L\|$ | 10⁵ |
| State $\sum n_\ell$ (at $n_\ell \approx$ 1024) | ~10⁸ dims ≈ 37 MB quinary |
| Operator blocks $\|L\|^2$ | **10¹⁰** |
| Sparsity mask alone (1 bit/block) | ~1.25 GB |

The state is free. The block grid is not. If you want the grid under 10⁹ blocks, you get $|L| \le \sim31{,}600$ labels ever — about **3 label mintings per year, forever**. That's a hard, checkable budget, and it's the number `ℒ_genesis` has to respect.

The spec's mitigation is "blocks default to zero matrices for absent relationships." That bounds *storage* of block contents, not the index, and it doesn't bound anything if label connectivity grows with $|L|$.

**Better conservation rule than the one I proposed last turn.** I said "merge or retire labels, or bound growth to $O(\log t)$." Retirement is the wrong lever — HSL v3.0 already has "automated archival of paradigms failing resonance checks" plus HDF5 frozen tiers, which demotes a label to cold storage without removing its row and column from the grid. Demotion doesn't shrink a quadratic.

The right lever is already in your own regime table: **"Block-diagonal projection in HTLA."** Bound the *connectivity degree* $k$ — the max number of other labels any label may have a nonzero block with. Then cost is $O(|L| \cdot k)$, linear, and labels can grow linearly forever. Genesis then costs: one new summand, plus at most $k$ new blocks, plus a mandatory declaration of which $k$ existing labels it couples to. If a new concept can't name its $k$ couplings, it isn't a concept yet — which is exactly the "a concept's content is its relational commitments" point from last turn, now with a price tag attached.

`ℒ_genesis` should therefore mint: (label id, dimension $n_\ell$, coupling set of size ≤ k, epoch, minting reason). Refuse the mint if the coupling set is empty or over budget.
